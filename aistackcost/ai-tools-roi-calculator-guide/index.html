How to use this guide

Start by naming the job you need AI to do: writing, research, video, coding, operations or design. Then compare tools by role instead of buying every popular subscription.

  • Pick one general assistant first.
  • Add specialists only when they remove real work.
  • Review monthly overlap before annual billing renews.

Budget decision framework

Lean stack: one general assistant plus one source-backed research tool.
Creator stack: writing, design and video tools only if they publish weekly.
Operator stack: autonomous agent tools only when delegated tasks replace owner hours.

Common mistake

The expensive mistake is paying for several generic assistants that all perform the same role. Use AIStackCost to map each tool to a job, seat count and monthly price before adding another subscription.

A concrete ROI example

Say a $20/month tool saves you 45 minutes a day, 5 days a week: that's roughly 15 hours a month. At a $40/hour rate, that's $600/month of time value against a $20 cost — an easy yes. The same $20 tool saving 20 minutes a week (~1.3 hours/month, ~$52 of value) is a much closer call, especially once you count the time spent learning and maintaining the workflow.

Frequently asked questions

What's a simple formula for AI tool ROI?

Estimate hours saved per month, multiply by your hourly rate (or salary equivalent), then subtract the tool's monthly cost. If the result is clearly positive after a month of real use, the subscription is paying for itself.

How long should I trial a tool before deciding?

About 30 days of real, typical-week usage. A single impressive session doesn't prove ongoing value; a full billing cycle of normal work does.

What if a tool saves time but I can't quantify revenue?

Time saved still counts. Convert hours saved per month into a rough dollar value using your hourly rate or salary, even if the tool isn't directly billable to a client.

Are prices guaranteed?

No. Prices change, so verify vendor pricing before buying.
